1 What is an LLM?

A Large Language Model (LLM) is a computer program that has read billions of pages of text — books, websites, code, conversations — and learned patterns of how words follow each other. When you ask it a question, it predicts what text should come next, word by word.

Think of it as a very advanced autocomplete. Your phone suggests the next word when you type a message. An LLM does the same thing, but at a much higher level — it can predict entire paragraphs that are coherent, factual-sounding, and contextually appropriate.

Examples: GPT-4o (by OpenAI), Claude (by Anthropic), Gemini (by Google), Cohere Command.

Key limitation: An LLM only knows what was in its training data. It has a "knowledge cutoff" — a date after which it knows nothing. It has never seen your company's internal documents, Jira tickets, or Slack messages.

🔗 Real-World Analogy: An LLM is like a brilliant new doctor who graduated top of their class and memorized every textbook — but has never seen YOUR medical file. They can talk impressively about any disease, but if you ask "what medication am I currently on?" they'll guess (sometimes dangerously wrong). RAG is like handing them your medical file before they diagnose you — now they're combining their expertise with YOUR specific data.

2 What are Tokens?

A token is the unit an LLM reads and generates. Tokens are NOT words — they're pieces of words. The LLM breaks text into tokens before processing it.

# How tokenization works (approximately):
"Employees receive 18 days of paid leave"
→ ["Employ", "ees", " receive", " 18", " days", " of", " paid", " leave"]
#   That's 8 tokens for 7 words

# Short common words = 1 token each: "the", "is", "and"
# Long/uncommon words = 2-3 tokens: "Employees" → "Employ" + "ees"
# Numbers = 1-2 tokens: "18" → 1 token, "2847" → might be 2

# ROUGH RULE: 1 token ≈ 4 characters ≈ 0.75 words
# So 1000 tokens ≈ 750 words ≈ 1.5 pages of text

Why tokens matter for RAG: Every LLM has a maximum number of tokens it can process at once (its "context window"). Your retrieved documents, system prompt, and the LLM's answer must all fit within this limit. Tokens also determine cost — API pricing is per-token.

3 What is a Context Window?

The context window is the total number of tokens an LLM can read + generate in one go. Think of it as the LLM's working memory — everything must fit inside it, or it literally cannot see it.

ModelContext WindowRoughly Equivalent To
GPT-4o-mini128K tokens~300 pages of text
Claude 3.5200K tokens~500 pages of text
Cohere Command128K tokens~300 pages

"300 pages sounds like a lot — why do I need RAG?" Because your company might have 10,000 pages of documentation. And even with large context windows, LLMs perform worse with more context — they miss information in the middle (the "lost in the middle" problem, covered in Tab ⑤). RAG means: instead of feeding all 10,000 pages, find the 3-5 most relevant paragraphs and feed only those.

🔗 Real-World Analogy: The context window is like a student's desk during an open-book exam. The desk can hold maybe 5-6 books open at once. If someone dumps 50 books on your desk, you'll spend the whole exam searching instead of answering. RAG is like a smart librarian who reads your question, walks to the shelves, and places exactly the 3 most relevant pages on your desk — instead of handing you the entire library catalog.

4 What is a Vector?

A vector is just a list of numbers. That's it. No complex math — it's a list.

# A vector:
[0.12, -0.45, 0.27, 0.88, -0.33]

# That's a vector with 5 numbers (5 "dimensions").
# Real embedding vectors have 384 to 3072 numbers.

Why lists of numbers? Because computers can't directly compare the meaning of text. "Work from home" and "remote work policy" look completely different as strings — different characters, different lengths. But if we convert each to a list of numbers where similar meaning produces similar numbers, the computer can compare them mathematically.

Think of it in 2D first: Imagine a map where every document is a dot. X-axis = "how much is this about HR?" Y-axis = "how much is this about IT?" An HR leave policy might be at position [0.9, 0.1]. A VPN troubleshooting guide might be at [0.1, 0.8]. A document about employee laptop replacements might be at [0.3, 0.7]. Documents about similar topics cluster together on this map.

Real embeddings do this same thing but with 384+ dimensions instead of 2. Each dimension captures some aspect of meaning (the model learns these aspects automatically during training — you don't choose them).

5 What is Cosine Similarity?

Once you have two vectors (two lists of numbers), you need a way to measure "how similar are they?" Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors.

Intuition: Imagine two arrows pointing from the same starting point. If they point in the same direction (angle ≈ 0°), they're very similar → cosine similarity ≈ 1.0. If they point in perpendicular directions (angle = 90°), they're unrelated → cosine similarity ≈ 0.0. If they point in opposite directions (angle = 180°), they're opposites → cosine similarity ≈ -1.0.

Score RangeWhat It MeansExample
0.85 – 1.0Very similar / near-duplicate"WFH policy" vs "work from home rules"
0.65 – 0.85Related / same topic"WFH policy" vs "office attendance requirements"
0.40 – 0.65Loosely related"WFH policy" vs "employee benefits overview"
0.0 – 0.40Unrelated"WFH policy" vs "cafeteria lunch timings"

Important: These ranges are approximate and vary by embedding model. MiniLM-L6-v2 (your course model) tends to produce lower similarity scores than OpenAI embeddings. Always test with your specific model and data.

⚠️ Score ≠ Percentage: A cosine similarity of 0.72 does NOT mean "72% relevant." It's a relative measure — useful for ranking (doc A scored 0.72, doc B scored 0.45, so A is more relevant than B) but not for absolute "good/bad" judgment. The Cohere rerank score from your notebook is also a ranking signal, not an accuracy percentage.

6 What is an API Call?

An API (Application Programming Interface) is how your code talks to an external service over the internet. When your Python notebook calls Cohere's reranking service or OpenAI's GPT model, it sends an HTTP request to their servers and gets a response back.

# What happens when you call co.rerank() in your notebook:

# 1. Your code packages the data:
#    "Here's a query and 5 documents, please rerank them"

# 2. Sends it over the internet to Cohere's server
#    (this is why you need an API key — it identifies you)

# 3. Cohere's server processes the request using their
#    reranking model (which runs on their GPUs, not yours)

# 4. Server sends back the result:
#    "Document 3 is most relevant (score 0.95),
#     Document 1 is second (score 0.71), ..."

# 5. Your code receives the response and uses it

# API key = your ID badge to access the service
# No key = the server rejects your request

Local vs. API models: HuggingFace's all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (your embedding model) runs locally — no API call, no internet needed, no cost. Cohere rerank and OpenAI GPT run remotely — API call required, internet needed, costs per use.

In LangChain (the framework your course uses), every piece of text is wrapped in a Document object. This is the atomic unit that flows through every RAG pipeline stage.

🔗 Real-World Analogy: A LangChain Document is like a library index card. The page_content is the actual text on the card — the book excerpt or summary. The metadata is the catalog information: author: "Smith", subject: "HR", shelf: "3A". Just like you filter library books by subject or author, you filter retrieved documents by metadata. And just like every library system (cataloging → shelving → lookup) operates on these cards, every RAG pipeline stage operates on Document objects.

2 When NOT to Use RAG

RAG adds complexity — retrieval, vector stores, chunking, embedding models, reranking. Sometimes simpler approaches are better:

Your entire knowledge base is < 50 pagesFull-context stuffing — paste everything into the promptModern LLMs handle 128K+ tokens. If it fits, searching is unnecessary overhead.
You want the LLM to write in a specific tone/styleFine-tuningRAG injects knowledge, not behavior. Style/tone is a model weights change.
The question is general knowledgeDirect LLM call"What is Python?" — the LLM already knows this. Retrieval adds noise.
Real-time data (stock prices, live scores)API tool callingRAG retrieves from static documents. Live data needs live API calls.
Simple FAQ with < 50 question-answer pairsRule-based lookup or embedding similarity onlyFull RAG pipeline is overkill for a small FAQ.

3 Chunking / Splitting — Deep Dive

Why chunk?

1. Embedding quality: Embedding a 50-page doc compresses all meaning into one vector. Embedding a focused paragraph captures specific meaning precisely — so similarity search actually works.

2. Context window: You can't send 50 pages to the LLM. You send only the 3-5 most relevant chunks.

Strategy 1: RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter (Most Common)

Splits by a hierarchy of separators: first tries to split on double-newlines (paragraph breaks), then single newlines, then sentences, then characters. This preserves natural text boundaries.

from langchain_text_splitters import RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter

splitter = RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter(
    chunk_size=500,      # max characters per chunk
    chunk_overlap=100,   # overlap prevents cutting mid-thought
    separators=["\n\n", "\n", ". ", " ", ""]  # try these in order
)
chunks = splitter.split_documents(pages)

# chunk_overlap=100 means:
# Chunk 1: "...the employee must submit the form within 15 days."
# Chunk 2: "submit the form within 15 days. The manager reviews..."
#          ↑ last 100 chars of chunk 1 repeat at start of chunk 2
# This prevents losing context at chunk boundaries
Document Typechunk_sizechunk_overlapWhy
Short policies (your notebook)No splitting needed—Each doc is already one focused fact
Long reports, manuals500–800100–150Balance precision and context
Legal documents1000–1500200–300Legal clauses need surrounding context
Code documentation300–50050–100Functions and classes are small units

Strategy 2: Parent-Child Chunking

The problem it solves

Small chunks = precise embeddings but incomplete context. Large chunks = complete context but diluted embeddings. Parent-child gets both: embed small, retrieve big.

# Concept:
# 1. Split doc into LARGE chunks (parents) — 2000 chars
# 2. Split each parent into SMALL chunks (children) — 400 chars
# 3. Embed and index only the CHILDREN
# 4. When a child matches a query, return its PARENT

# Why this works:
# Small child: "Refund requests must be submitted within 30 days."
#   → precise embedding, matches "refund deadline" queries
#
# But the LLM receives the parent (which contains):
#   "Our refund policy covers all purchases made through the
#    official store. Refund requests must be submitted within
#    30 days. Processing takes 5-7 business days. The refund
#    is credited to the original payment method."
#   → full context for a complete answer

from langchain.retrievers import ParentDocumentRetriever
from langchain.storage import InMemoryStore
from langchain_text_splitters import RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter

parent_splitter = RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter(chunk_size=2000)
child_splitter = RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter(chunk_size=400)

store = InMemoryStore()  # stores parent documents

retriever = ParentDocumentRetriever(
    vectorstore=vector_store,     # children go here (for search)
    docstore=store,               # parents go here (for retrieval)
    child_splitter=child_splitter,
    parent_splitter=parent_splitter,
)
retriever.add_documents(pages)

# Now: search matches children, but returns parents!
results = retriever.invoke("refund deadline")
# Returns the full parent chunk with complete context
🔗 Real-World Analogy: Parent-child chunking is like a textbook's index at the back. The index entry (child) is compact and searchable: "Refund policy — page 47." But when you flip to page 47, you read the full section with all the context (parent). The index is small for fast lookup; the actual page gives you the complete picture. Embed the index entries, retrieve the full pages.

Strategy 3: Semantic Chunking

Instead of splitting by character count, split where the meaning changes. Adjacent sentences that are about the same topic stay together; a shift in topic triggers a split.

from langchain_experimental.text_splitter import SemanticChunker

semantic_splitter = SemanticChunker(
    embeddings=embeddings,  # uses your embedding model
    breakpoint_threshold_type="percentile",
    breakpoint_threshold_amount=90  # split at top 10% meaning shifts
)
# How it works:
# 1. Embed each sentence individually
# 2. Compare cosine similarity between adjacent sentences
# 3. Where similarity drops sharply → that's a topic boundary → split

# Example:
# Sentence 1: "Employees get 18 days of leave."     } similar
# Sentence 2: "Leave requires manager approval."     } → keep together
# Sentence 3: "Password resets use the IT portal."   } ← big meaning shift → SPLIT HERE

Trade-off: More intelligent splits but requires running the embedding model during chunking (slower than character splitting). Best for documents with varied topics in the same file.

1 Two Worlds of Search

Every search system matches either words (keyword search) or meaning (semantic search). Understanding both — and why you need both — is the core of RAG retrieval.

🔗 Real-World Analogy: Keyword search = searching your email for the exact word "invoice." Only finds emails containing that exact word — misses one titled "payment receipt" even though it's the same thing. Semantic search = asking a smart assistant "find me anything about money I owe." It understands the meaning and finds emails about invoices, bills, payments, and due balances — even though none of them share the same words. You need both: exact search for known terms, semantic search for finding things described differently.

2 BM25 — Keyword Search Algorithm

BM25 scores each document by asking three questions:

Q1 — Term Frequency (TF): "How often does the search word appear in THIS document?" More mentions = more likely relevant. But BM25 has a saturation curve — going from 1 to 3 matters a lot; 30 to 32 barely matters.

Q2 — Inverse Document Frequency (IDF): "How rare is this word across ALL documents?" "employee" appears in every doc → useless for distinguishing. "VPN-403" appears in one doc → very strong signal. Rare words carry more weight.

Q3 — Document Length: A 50-word policy mentioning "refund" once is more focused than a 5000-word handbook mentioning it once. BM25 normalizes by document length.

🔗 Real-World Analogy — Restaurant Reviews: TF = how many times a reviewer mentions "spicy" in their review (frequency — they really mean it). IDF = how rarely do ANY reviewers across ALL restaurants mention "truffle oil"? If only 2 out of 500 reviews mention it, that word is a strong signal for finding those specific restaurants. Length normalization = a 3-sentence review mentioning "spicy" once is more focused than a 3-page review mentioning it once. Rare, repeated words in short reviews carry the strongest signal — exactly like BM25. 
# BM25 internals — what the formula is actually computing:
def bm25_score(query, document, all_documents):
    score = 0
    k1 = 1.5   # controls TF saturation
    b = 0.75   # controls length normalization (0=none, 1=full)
    avg_len = average(len(d) for d in all_documents)
    
    for word in tokenize(query):
        tf = count(word in document)
        docs_with_word = count(d for d in all_documents if word in d)
        
        idf = log((len(all_documents) - docs_with_word + 0.5) /
                  (docs_with_word + 0.5) + 1)
        
        length_norm = 1 - b + b * (len(document) / avg_len)
        term_score = idf * (tf * (k1 + 1)) / (tf + k1 * length_norm)
        score += term_score
    return score
⚠️ Tokenization is critical: Your notebook's regex r"[a-z0-9]+(?:-[a-z0-9]+)*" preserves "VPN-403" as one token. Without it, "VPN-403" splits into ["vpn", "403"] and matching breaks. Always test your tokenizer with your data's edge cases: error codes, product IDs, hyphenated terms.

3 Dense / Semantic Search

How it works

1. Your embedding model converts the user's query into a vector (list of numbers). 2. The vector store compares this query vector against all stored document vectors using cosine similarity. 3. Returns the top-K most similar documents.

Bi-Encoder vs. Cross-Encoder

Bi-Encoder (retrieval): Encodes query and document INDEPENDENTLY, then compares. Fast — document vectors are pre-computed. But misses subtle word interactions.

Cross-Encoder (reranking): Processes query AND document TOGETHER as one input. Much more accurate — sees how specific query words relate to specific document words. But slow — can't pre-compute, must run per pair.

Strategy: Bi-encoder retrieves top 10-20 quickly. Cross-encoder reranks those candidates precisely.

🔗 Real-World Analogy: Bi-encoder = a job portal matching resumes to job descriptions by comparing their tags independently (fast — tags are pre-computed, but misses subtle fits). Cross-encoder = a hiring manager reading one specific resume alongside one specific job description together, seeing that "managed a 4-person team" perfectly fits "seeking team lead experience" (slow — must do this for each resume-job pair, but catches nuances the tags miss).

RRF (Reciprocal Rank Fusion)

# fusion_score = Σ (weight / (c + rank))

# c=60 (standard), weights=[0.5, 0.5] (equal)

2 Query Rewriting

Users ask differently from how documents are written. An LLM reformulates the query to better match document language.

def rewrite_query(original, llm):
    prompt = f"""Rewrite this question to use formal business
    language that would appear in company policy documents.
    Return ONLY the rewritten query.
    
    Original: {original}"""
    return llm.invoke(prompt).content

# "how do I get my money back?" → "What is the refund and return policy?"
# "my VPN is broken"            → "VPN access troubleshooting and error resolution"
🔗 Real-World Analogy: Like when you tell a pharmacist "I need something for my headache" and they translate it in their head to the medical term "analgesic — oral, non-prescription, adult dose" before searching their inventory system. Your casual language isn't how their database is organized — the rewrite bridges that gap.

3 Multi-Query Expansion

One query phrasing might miss relevant docs. Generate 3-5 variations, retrieve for each, merge results.

from langchain.retrievers.multi_query import MultiQueryRetriever

multi_retriever = MultiQueryRetriever.from_llm(
    retriever=vector_store.as_retriever(search_kwargs={"k": 3}),
    llm=llm
)
# User: "Can I work from home?"
# LLM generates internally:
#   1. "What is the remote work policy?"
#   2. "Is telecommuting allowed?"  
#   3. "Work from home eligibility"
# Retrieves for ALL, deduplicates, returns union
results = multi_retriever.invoke("Can I work from home?")

Trade-off: 3-5× more retrieval calls but dramatically better recall.

4 HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings)

The Problem

Questions and documents live in different "language spaces." The question "Can I work from home during my first week?" is phrased as a question. But the document says "Employees must work from the office during their first 30 days." The embedding of a question and the embedding of a declarative statement might not be as close as you'd want.

The Solution

Ask the LLM to generate a hypothetical answer — a fake document that WOULD answer the question. Then embed that fake document instead of the question. The fake answer is in "document language," so it's closer in embedding space to the real documents.

# HyDE step-by-step:
def hyde_retrieve(query, llm, retriever):
    # Step 1: Generate hypothetical answer
    hyde_prompt = f"""Write a short paragraph that would answer 
    this question, as if it were from a company policy document.
    Don't worry about accuracy — this is for search purposes only.
    
    Question: {query}"""
    
    hypothetical_doc = llm.invoke(hyde_prompt).content
    # "Employees are required to work from the office for the first
    #  30 days of employment. Remote work privileges begin after the
    #  initial onboarding period is completed."
    
    # Step 2: Embed the hypothetical answer (not the question!)
    # Step 3: Search for real documents similar to the hypothetical
    results = retriever.invoke(hypothetical_doc)
    # The hypothetical sounds like a policy → matches real policies!
    
    return results
🔗 Real-World Analogy: HyDE is like when you can't remember the name of a song, so instead of humming it badly to Shazam, you describe what the song would sound like: "It's a 2010s pop ballad with piano intro, female vocalist singing about leaving a small town." Then you Google THAT description — it matches music reviews and blog posts much better than your humming would. You're searching with "answer language" instead of "question language."
⚠️ HyDE trade-off: Requires an LLM call BEFORE retrieval (adds latency + cost). The hypothetical answer might be wrong — but that's okay because you're only using its embedding for search, not its content for the answer. The real documents provide the actual answer.

6 Post-Retrieval Filtering

After retrieval, apply additional quality checks on the results:

# 1. Score threshold — remove low-relevance results
def filter_by_score(docs, scores, threshold=0.3):
    return [(d, s) for d, s in zip(docs, scores) if s >= threshold]

# 2. Deduplication — remove near-identical chunks
def deduplicate(docs, threshold=0.95):
    unique = [docs[0]]
    for doc in docs[1:]:
        if not any(cosine_sim(doc, kept) > threshold for kept in unique):
            unique.append(doc)
    return unique

# 3. Business rules — only active, non-archived docs
def apply_rules(docs):
    return [d for d in docs if d.metadata.get("status") != "archived"]

Pre vs. Post: Pre-filtering is faster (reduces search space) but needs metadata in the vector store. Post-filtering is more flexible (complex logic) but wastes compute on docs you'll discard. Use both for different purposes.

"Lost in the Middle" — Why Reordering Exists

Research shows LLMs pay most attention to the START and END of context, and often miss the MIDDLE. Reordering places the most relevant docs at positions where the LLM pays attention:

from langchain_community.document_transformers import LongContextReorder

reorder = LongContextReorder()
reordered = reorder.transform_documents(results)
# Input  (by relevance): [best, good, okay, decent, meh]
# Output (reordered):    [best, okay, meh, decent, good]
#                         ^^^^                     ^^^^
#                       start (high attention)   end (high attention)
# Middle gets least relevant — where LLM attention is weakest
🔗 Real-World Analogy: Reranking = a teacher grading exam papers and keeping only the top 3 (decides WHICH papers make the cut). Reordering = arranging those 3 papers on the principal's desk — best paper on top, second-best at bottom, weakest in the middle — because the principal reads the first and last most carefully and skims the middle. Same papers, arranged for maximum reader attention.

3 Token Budget

Even with 128K tokens available, more context ≠ better answers. Sweet spot: 3-5 well-chosen chunks (2000-5000 tokens). The "lost in the middle" problem gets worse with more context. Use temperature=0 for factual Q&A.

Checkpoint 1 — Retrieve Token: "Do I need external knowledge?"

"What is 2+2?" → No retrieval needed (general knowledge). "What's our refund policy?" → Yes, retrieve (company-specific).

Checkpoint 2 — IsRelevant: "Are these documents useful?"

After generation, verify: "Would someone reading these same documents reach the same conclusion?" If not, regenerate or flag uncertainty.

🔗 Real-World Analogy: Self-RAG is like a careful doctor. Checkpoint 1 = "Does this patient even need a blood test, or is this clearly a common cold?" (Don't order unnecessary tests.) Checkpoint 2 = "The lab results came back — are these results actually for THIS patient and relevant to THIS symptom?" (Don't read someone else's report.) Checkpoint 3 = "My diagnosis matches what the test results show, right?" (Don't prescribe treatment that contradicts the evidence.) At each stage, stop and verify instead of blindly continuing.

2 Corrective RAG (CRAG)

Grades retrieval quality and takes corrective action for each outcome:

CORRECT — docs clearly relevant → generate normally.

AMBIGUOUS — docs partially related → rewrite query → re-retrieve.

INCORRECT — docs completely irrelevant → fall back to web search or acknowledge gap.

🔗 Real-World Analogy: CRAG works like a customer support center. Correct = the FAQ has the exact answer, support agent reads it out (Level 1). Ambiguous = the FAQ is close but doesn't quite match — agent rephrases the customer's question and searches again with better terms (Level 2). Incorrect = the FAQ has nothing relevant — agent escalates to a specialist or checks an external resource (Level 3). Each grade triggers a different action instead of blindly giving a wrong answer.

3 Query Routing

When you have multiple knowledge bases, route each query to the right one instead of searching all of them.

def route_query(query, llm):
    """Classify query → route to correct vectorstore."""
    routing_prompt = f"""Classify this question into ONE category:
    - HR (leave, WFH, benefits, onboarding)
    - IT (VPN, password, laptop, software)
    - FINANCE (travel expenses, reimbursement, budget)
    Reply with ONLY the category name.
    
    Question: {query}"""
    
    category = llm.invoke(routing_prompt).content.strip().upper()
    
    # Each category has its own vector store
    stores = {
        "HR": hr_vectorstore.as_retriever(search_kwargs={"k": 3}),
        "IT": it_vectorstore.as_retriever(search_kwargs={"k": 3}),
        "FINANCE": finance_vectorstore.as_retriever(search_kwargs={"k": 3}),
    }
    
    retriever = stores.get(category, stores["HR"])  # default to HR
    return retriever.invoke(query)

# "How many leave days?" → HR → searches only HR docs
# "VPN-403 error"        → IT → searches only IT docs
# "meal reimbursement"   → FINANCE → searches only finance docs

Why not search everything? Searching all stores returns noise from irrelevant domains. An IT doc mentioning "leave the VPN connected" might match a leave policy query via the word "leave." Routing prevents this cross-domain pollution.

🔗 Real-World Analogy: Query routing is like a hospital reception desk. A patient with a broken arm goes to Orthopedics, not Cardiology. A patient with chest pain goes to Cardiology, not Dermatology. Without routing, every department gets every patient → doctors are overwhelmed with irrelevant cases → real emergencies get delayed. The receptionist (router) classifies the problem and sends it to the right specialist (knowledge base).

1 Vector Databases — How They Work

Normal databases index for exact lookups ("find row where id=42"). Vector databases index for similarity lookups ("find the 5 vectors closest to this query vector"). With 1 million vectors, brute-force comparison is too slow — vector DBs use Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) algorithms.

ANN Index Types

HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World): Builds a graph of connected neighbors. Search navigates the graph toward the query. Used by Qdrant, Pinecone, Chroma. Fast and accurate.

IVF (Inverted File Index): Clusters vectors into groups (k-means). Searches only nearest clusters. Used by FAISS. Very fast but needs periodic re-clustering.

Flat (Brute Force): Compares every vector. 100% accurate but O(n). Fine for <10K vectors (your learning projects).

🔗 Real-World Analogy: Finding a specific book in a library. Brute force = walking through every shelf in the entire library checking every book (accurate but takes hours). IVF = going to the right floor and right section first, then scanning only that area (fast, might miss a book filed in a related section). HNSW = asking a librarian who points you to a shelf, and a sign on that shelf points to a nearby related shelf — you hop between connected shelves until you find the exact book (fast and accurate).

3 Normalization

Your notebook uses normalize_embeddings=True. Normalization scales every vector to unit length (magnitude=1). Without it, longer texts might produce "bigger" vectors, skewing similarity. After normalization, cosine similarity and dot product (which is faster) give the same result.

1 Why Evaluation Matters

Without evaluation, your RAG pipeline is a black box. You build it, ask a few questions, the answers look reasonable — and you ship it. Then in production, it confidently gives wrong answers and nobody notices because the answers SOUND right.

Evaluation answers: "Is my pipeline actually finding the right documents? Is the LLM actually using them correctly?"

⚠️ The biggest mistake in production RAG: Not measuring retrieval quality. You can trace LLM outputs in LangSmith all day, but if the retrieved chunks are wrong, the LLM will generate confident, well-formatted WRONG answers. Measure retrieval first.

2 RAGAS — The Standard Evaluation Framework

RAGAS (Retrieval-Augmented Generation Assessment) defines four core metrics. Each catches a different type of failure:

Metric 1: Faithfulness

Question: "Is the generated answer actually supported by the retrieved context?"

Catches: Hallucination — the LLM adds facts not present in the retrieved documents.

Example: Context says "Refund within 30 days." Answer says "Refund within 30 days, and you'll receive store credit for items over 90 days." The store credit part is fabricated → low faithfulness.

# Faithfulness = (claims supported by context) / (total claims in answer)
# Score range: 0.0 (fully hallucinated) to 1.0 (fully grounded)

Metric 2: Answer Relevancy

Question: "Does the answer actually address what the user asked?"

Catches: Off-topic answers — the LLM found context and answered, but about the wrong thing.

Example: User asks "How many sick days?" Answer talks about annual leave policies. The answer is factual and grounded but doesn't answer the actual question → low answer relevancy.

Metric 3: Context Precision

Question: "Of the documents I retrieved, how many were actually relevant?"

Catches: Noisy retrieval — you retrieved 5 docs but only 1 was useful.

Example: For "How many sick days?", you retrieve: D2 (sick leave — relevant), D1 (annual leave — wrong type), D11 (cafeteria hours — irrelevant). Only 1 of 3 is actually relevant → low context precision.

# Context Precision = (relevant retrieved docs) / (total retrieved docs)
# High precision → your retrieval is selective, not noisy

Metric 4: Context Recall

Question: "Did I find ALL the relevant documents, or did I miss some?"

Catches: Incomplete retrieval — the answer is correct as far as it goes, but misses important information in documents the retriever didn't return.

Example: You have docs about sick leave (D2) AND a doc about extended sick leave for hospitalization (D15). Your retriever found D2 but missed D15. The answer is incomplete → low context recall.

# Context Recall = (relevant docs retrieved) / (total relevant docs that exist)
# High recall → you're not missing critical information
🔗 Real-World Analogy: Imagine a fishing net. Context precision = "of all the fish I caught, how many are the species I actually wanted?" If I caught 10 fish but only 2 are tuna and 8 are random bycatch, my precision is 0.2 — too much junk. Context recall = "of all the tuna in the ocean (that I know about), how many did I actually catch?" If there were 5 tuna in the area and I caught 3, my recall is 0.6 — I missed 2. You need both: high precision (mostly the right fish) AND high recall (don't miss the ones that matter).

Program 6: Self-RAG (3 Checkpoints) 
# ═══ SELF-RAG — Validate retrieval and answer ═══
def self_rag(query, retriever, llm, verbose=True):
    # Checkpoint 1: Need retrieval?
    c1 = llm.invoke(f"Does '{query}' need company docs? Reply 'retrieve' or 'general'.").content.strip().lower()
    if verbose: print(f"CP1: {c1}")
    if "general" in c1: return llm.invoke(query).content
    
    docs = retriever.invoke(query)
    if verbose: print(f"  Retrieved {len(docs)} docs")
    
    # Checkpoint 2: Docs relevant?
    relevant = []
    for doc in docs:
        c2 = llm.invoke(f"Is this relevant to '{query}'? Reply 'relevant'/'irrelevant'.\nDoc: {doc.page_content}").content.strip().lower()
        if verbose: print(f"  CP2 {doc.metadata.get('id')}: {c2}")
        if "relevant" in c2: relevant.append(doc)
    
    if not relevant: return "I couldn't find relevant information."
    
    answer = generate(query, relevant)
    
    # Checkpoint 3: Supported?
    ctx = make_context(relevant)
    c3 = llm.invoke(f"Is this answer supported by context? Reply 'supported'/'unsupported'.\nContext: {ctx}\nAnswer: {answer}").content.strip().lower()
    if verbose: print(f"  CP3: {c3}")
    if "unsupported" in c3: return f"Unverified. Raw docs:\n{ctx}"
    return answer

print(self_rag("How many sick days?", hybrid, llm))
print(self_rag("What is 2+2?", hybrid, llm))
